\begin{abstract}
Fixed-time and vehicle-actuated traffic signal controllers remain the de-facto
standard at urban intersections, yet both lose efficiency under asymmetric and
time-varying demand because their control logic is not learned from traffic
dynamics. This paper presents an adaptive signal controller based on a
Dueling Double Deep Q-Network (D3QN) that selects signal phases from a
discretized, normalized intersection state and is trained entirely in the
microscopic simulator SUMO through the TraCI interface. Signal control is cast
as a discrete phase-selection Markov Decision Process with a shaped, bounded
reward centred on reducing cumulative vehicle waiting time; minimum-green and
yellow/all-red clearance are enforced by the environment to guarantee physically
valid, safe signal plans. Evaluated against fixed-time and actuated baselines on
three demand regimes---balanced, asymmetric, and a mid-episode surge---each
replicated over three random seeds and reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation,
the D3QN reduces average waiting time by 75.5--82.6\% relative to fixed-time
control and by 18.1--38.0\% relative to a strong actuated baseline, while
matching throughput and reducing estimated CO\textsubscript{2} emissions by
nearly an order of magnitude. We further address the common homogeneous-fleet
assumption by introducing a Passenger Car Unit (PCU) weighting (IRC~106-1990)
into both state and reward, evaluated on a mixed fleet (car, motorcycle,
auto-rickshaw, bus) under SUMO's sub-lane lateral model. An ablation shows PCU
weighting lowers waiting time by 23.9\% and halves run-to-run variance relative
to an identical unweighted agent. All code, trained models, and experiments are
reproducible via a single containerized command.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Traffic congestion at signalized intersections imposes substantial costs in
travel-time delay, fuel consumption, and vehicular emissions. The signal
controller---the logic that decides which movements receive right of way and for
how long---is the single most influential control element at an isolated
intersection. Two control paradigms dominate practice. \emph{Fixed-time} (or
pre-timed) control executes a fixed cyclic schedule of phases with pre-computed
durations, derived offline from historical demand. \emph{Vehicle-actuated}
control uses loop or virtual detectors to extend a green phase while vehicles
continue to arrive within a critical time gap, subject to minimum and maximum
green bounds. Fixed-time control is simple and predictable but blind to live
conditions; actuated control adapts locally but greedily, optimizing only the
immediate gap rather than any long-horizon objective, and its performance is
sensitive to detector placement and gap settings.

Reinforcement learning (RL) reframes signal control as sequential
decision-making: an agent observes the intersection state, selects a control
action, receives a reward reflecting traffic efficiency, and improves its policy
through experience. Unlike hand-tuned heuristics, an RL agent can, in principle,
learn to anticipate the downstream consequences of a phase decision and thereby
minimize a cumulative objective such as total delay. Deep Q-Networks
(DQN)~\cite{mnih2015} made this tractable for high-dimensional states by
approximating the action-value function with a neural network stabilized by
experience replay and a target network. Subsequent refinements---Double
DQN~\cite{vanhasselt2016}, which corrects the systematic overestimation of
action values, and the Dueling architecture~\cite{wang2016}, which separates the
estimation of state value from action advantage---improve both stability and
sample efficiency. The combination, often termed D3QN, is a natural fit for the
modest state and action dimensionality of an isolated intersection.

Despite a large body of work applying DQN-family methods to signal control,
three practical shortcomings recur and motivate this study:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Insufficient baselines.} Many studies benchmark only against
fixed-time control, an intentionally weak comparator, and omit the far stronger
actuated controller. The reported improvement then overstates the practical
benefit of the learned policy.
\item \textbf{Homogeneous-traffic assumption.} State representations frequently
count raw vehicles, implicitly assigning equal weight to a motorcycle and a bus.
For mixed fleets---prevalent in many regions---this misrepresents the true
traffic load an approach imposes on the intersection.
\item \textbf{Limited reproducibility.} Single-run results without random-seed
replication or a portable execution environment are difficult to trust and
harder to reproduce.
\end{enumerate}

This paper directly targets all three. We benchmark against both fixed-time and
actuated control; we introduce a PCU-weighted state and reward and evaluate it on
an explicitly heterogeneous fleet; and we package the entire pipeline in a
container with multi-seed statistical reporting.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
\item A compact Dueling Double DQN controller for an isolated four-way
intersection in which the environment---not the agent---enforces minimum-green
and mandatory yellow/all-red clearance, guaranteeing physically valid signal
plans irrespective of the policy output.
\item A shaped, bounded reward with per-component logging that makes the drivers
of the agent's behaviour transparent and debuggable.
\item A rigorous multi-seed evaluation against fixed-time and actuated baselines
over three demand regimes, measuring waiting time, queue length, throughput, and
estimated emissions.
\item A PCU-weighted state and reward formulation for heterogeneous traffic, with
an ablation that isolates and quantifies its contribution.
\item A one-command, Docker-based reproducible artifact releasing code, trained
models, and experiment scripts.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work and Research Gaps}
\label{sec:related}
Learning-based signal control typically couples the microscopic simulator
SUMO~\cite{sumo2018} with the TraCI control interface and a value-based or
policy-gradient agent. The value-based line builds on the DQN~\cite{mnih2015},
whose two central stabilizers---an experience replay buffer that decorrelates
consecutive transitions, and a slowly updated target network that provides a
stationary bootstrap target---remain standard. Double DQN~\cite{vanhasselt2016}
addresses the maximization bias inherent in the single-network target by using
the online network to \emph{select} the greedy next action and the target
network to \emph{evaluate} it. The Dueling architecture~\cite{wang2016}
decomposes the action-value into a state-value term and an
advantage term, improving learning when the choice of action is
inconsequential in many states---a frequent situation in signal control, where
holding a green during light demand is often as good as any alternative.
Standardized environment wrappers such as SUMO-RL~\cite{sumorl} have lowered the
barrier to experimentation.

Against this backdrop, the present work is distinguished on three axes.
\emph{First}, on baseline rigour: we retain SUMO's native actuated controller as
a strong comparator rather than reporting only against fixed-time control.
\emph{Second}, on heterogeneity: rather than counting vehicles uniformly, we
embed IRC~106-1990 Passenger Car Unit factors~\cite{irc106} into the learned
representation, so the agent perceives traffic \emph{load} rather than vehicle
\emph{count}. This is framed as a DQN-specific extension of the broader
heterogeneous-traffic control literature. \emph{Third}, on reproducibility: we
report mean~$\pm$~standard deviation across multiple seeds and ship a
containerized pipeline that regenerates every table and figure from a single
command.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
\subsection{Markov Decision Process}
We model signal control as a Markov Decision Process (MDP)
$(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma)$, where $\mathcal{S}$ is the state
space, $\mathcal{A}$ the discrete action space, $P(s'\!\mid s,a)$ the transition
kernel induced by the traffic dynamics, $r(s,a)$ the reward, and
$\gamma \in [0,1)$ the discount factor. The agent seeks a policy
$\pi:\mathcal{S}\!\rightarrow\!\mathcal{A}$ maximizing the expected discounted
return $\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t\geq0}\gamma^{t} r_t\right]$.

\subsection{From Q-Learning to D3QN}
The optimal action-value function obeys the Bellman optimality equation
\begin{equation}
Q^{*}(s,a) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[ r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q^{*}(s',a') \,\middle|\, s,a \right].
\end{equation}
DQN approximates $Q^{*}$ by a network $Q_\theta$ trained to minimize the temporal
difference error against a target computed from a periodically frozen copy
$Q_{\theta^-}$. Double DQN replaces the $\max$ operator with a decoupled
selection/evaluation:
\begin{equation}
y = r + \gamma\, Q_{\theta^-}\!\big(s', \arg\max_{a'} Q_\theta(s',a')\big),
\label{eq:ddqn}
\end{equation}
reducing overestimation. The Dueling network parameterizes
\begin{equation}
Q_\theta(s,a) = V_\theta(s) + \Big( A_\theta(s,a) - \tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\!\sum_{a'} A_\theta(s,a') \Big),
\label{eq:dueling}
\end{equation}
where the mean-subtraction term resolves the additive ambiguity between $V$ and
$A$ and stabilizes optimization. Equations~\eqref{eq:ddqn}--\eqref{eq:dueling}
together constitute the D3QN used here.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Simulation Environment and Network}
Experiments use Eclipse SUMO~1.27.1 in headless mode, controlled from Python via
TraCI. The intersection is a single four-way junction generated with SUMO's
\texttt{netconvert} from plain node/edge/connection descriptions, ensuring a
valid internal-lane geometry. Each of the four approaches has two incoming and
two outgoing lanes of 200~m length with a 13.89~m/s (50~km/h) free-flow speed.
Left turns are permissive (gap-acceptance), which keeps the control problem to a
compact two-phase North--South / East--West structure and avoids protected-turn
signal conflicts. A fixed connection ordering is imposed so that the 16-link
signal state string is consistent across the static and actuated programs.

\subsection{Demand Scenarios}
Four demand regimes are used. \emph{Balanced} loads all four approaches roughly
equally at $\sim$300~veh/h each. \emph{Asymmetric} loads the North--South
corridor heavily and the East--West corridor lightly, a regime under which
fixed-time control visibly wastes green on the light approaches. \emph{Surge}
begins at a moderate uniform rate and ramps the North--South demand sharply at
mid-episode, testing temporal adaptability. \emph{Heterogeneous} replaces the
uniform passenger-car fleet with a mixture of vehicle classes simulated under
SUMO's SL2015 sub-lane model (lateral resolution 0.8~m), enabling realistic
lateral behaviour such as motorcycle filtering.

\subsection{State Representation}
The state is a 71-dimensional vector normalized to $[0,1]$, summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:state}. For each of the eight incoming lanes we encode a
three-cell occupancy (density) profile and a three-cell mean-speed profile
measured from the stop line backward along the lane, capturing both how many
vehicles are present and how fast they move at different distances. We append the
per-lane halting-vehicle queue length and per-lane cumulative waiting time, a
six-dimensional one-hot encoding of the active signal phase, and a binary flag
indicating whether the minimum-green interval has elapsed (informing the agent
whether a switch is currently permissible).

\subsection{Action Space and Safety Wrapper}
The action space is discrete with $|\mathcal{A}|=2$: action $0$ requests the
North--South green phase and action $1$ the East--West green phase, with a
decision taken every five simulated seconds. Crucially, the environment enforces
operational safety independently of the agent: a ten-second minimum green must
elapse before a switch is honoured, and any change of the served axis
automatically inserts a three-second yellow interval followed by a two-second
all-red clearance before the new green is displayed. Consequently every policy
the agent can express maps to a physically valid, safe signal plan.

\subsection{Reward Function}
The reward is shaped and bounded:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r_t = &\;(W_{t-1} - W_t)
      - \lambda_s\,\mathbb{1}[a_t \neq a_{t-1}]
      - \lambda_q \sum_{l} q_{l,t}, \\
r_t \leftarrow &\; \mathrm{clip}(r_t,\,-R,\,+R),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $W_t$ is the total cumulative waiting time across incoming lanes,
$q_{l,t}$ the queue on lane $l$, $\lambda_s=0.5$ the switching penalty that
discourages phase flicker, $\lambda_q=0.1$ the queue-penalty weight, and $R=10$
the clip bound that keeps the target magnitude bounded for stable Q-learning.
The primary term rewards \emph{reductions} in waiting time, directing the agent
toward decongestion rather than reacting to absolute delay. Each term in
\eqref{eq:reward} is logged separately per step, allowing the contribution of
each component to be inspected during and after training.

\subsection{Agent Architecture and Training}
The Q-network is a fully connected trunk of two 128-unit ReLU layers feeding two
heads: a scalar value stream and a two-output advantage stream, combined by
\eqref{eq:dueling}. Targets follow the Double-DQN rule \eqref{eq:ddqn}. Training
uses an experience replay buffer of capacity 50\,000, a target network
hard-updated every 500 gradient steps, $\varepsilon$-greedy exploration decaying
linearly from 1.0 to 0.05, the Huber (smooth-L1) loss, and gradient-norm
clipping at 10. Optimization uses Adam with learning rate $5\times10^{-4}$,
discount $\gamma=0.99$, and minibatch size 64. Algorithm~\ref{alg:train}
summarizes the training loop.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{D3QN training loop (per episode)}
\label{alg:train}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State reset SUMO episode; observe initial state $s$
\While{episode not done}
  \State $a \gets \varepsilon$-greedy action from $Q_\theta(s,\cdot)$
  \State environment applies $a$ with min-green + yellow/all-red;
  \State \quad advance simulation by the decision interval
  \State observe $s'$, reward $r$ from \eqref{eq:reward}, done flag $d$
  \State store $(s,a,r,s',d)$ in replay buffer
  \If{buffer $\geq$ batch size}
     \State sample minibatch; compute targets by \eqref{eq:ddqn}
     \State gradient step on Huber loss; clip gradients
     \State decay $\varepsilon$; periodically update $\theta^- \gets \theta$
  \EndIf
  \State $s \gets s'$
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{PCU-Weighted Representation for Heterogeneous Traffic}
Under mixed traffic, a raw halting count misrepresents the load an approach
imposes: a queued bus occupies far more effective road space and takes longer to
discharge than a motorcycle. We therefore weight each vehicle's contribution to
the queue features (in the state) and to the queue penalty (in the reward) by its
Passenger Car Unit factor~\cite{irc106}: car $=1.0$, motorcycle $=0.5$,
auto-rickshaw $=0.8$, and bus $=3.0$. Formally, the PCU-weighted queue on lane
$l$ is $\tilde q_{l,t} = \sum_{v \in \text{halting}(l)} \mathrm{PCU}(\text{class}(v))$,
substituted for $q_{l,t}$ in both the state and in \eqref{eq:reward}. Raw counts
are retained separately for reporting so that cross-controller metric comparisons
remain fair.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
Evaluation episodes span 1200 simulated seconds. Agents are trained for 80
episodes on the homogeneous scenarios and 150 on the heterogeneous scenario, with
the $\varepsilon$-decay schedule matched to the corresponding training budget so
that exploration anneals well before the exploitation phase. The complete
software stack---SUMO, TraCI, PyTorch~2.14 (CPU), and supporting
libraries---executes inside a single Docker image built on Ubuntu~22.04, giving
identical behaviour across machines. The learned policy is evaluated greedily
($\varepsilon=0$). Every configuration is repeated over seeds $\{1,2,3\}$ and
reported as mean~$\pm$~standard deviation.

\subsection{Heterogeneous Traffic: PCU Ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:pcu} and Fig.~\ref{fig:pcu} compare controllers on the mixed-fleet

vehicles proportionally, the weighted agent learns a more consistent and
effective policy. At the training budget used, however, the PCU-weighted agent
does not surpass the actuated baseline on heterogeneous traffic; narrowing this
remaining gap is identified as future work.

\subsection{On the Scientific Process}
An initial short heterogeneous run (60 episodes, PCU applied to the state only)
produced the opposite conclusion, with PCU weighting appearing harmful. Diagnosis
attributed this to under-training and to the fact that the reward objective was
unchanged, so the weighting altered only the observation scale. A corrected
protocol---applying PCU to both state and reward, training for 150 episodes, and
retuning the $\varepsilon$-decay for the harder scenario---reversed the finding to
the positive result of Table~\ref{tab:pcu}. Both outcomes are reported to document
the process transparently rather than presenting only the favourable run.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discuss}
Three observations follow from the results. First, a compact D3QN not only beats
fixed-time control by a wide margin but also outperforms a well-configured
actuated controller on delay and queueing, without sacrificing throughput; the
learned policy allocates green time in anticipation of accumulating demand rather
than reacting to the immediate gap. Second, delegating minimum-green and
clearance enforcement to the environment cleanly separates \emph{what to do}
(the learned policy) from \emph{what is safe} (the fixed wrapper), so that every
policy remains deployable in principle. Third, the PCU ablation substantiates the
central claim of the heterogeneous extension: \emph{what} the agent counts
matters. Weighting the state and reward by vehicle class produces a better and
markedly more stable policy than uniform counting, which is directly relevant to
regions where mixed traffic is the norm.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limit}
The study concerns a single isolated intersection; network-level coordination,
which introduces multi-agent non-stationarity, is out of scope. The evaluation is
simulation-only, uses permissive (not protected) left turns, and a discretized
state. The training budget---1200~s episodes and 80--150 training
episodes---was chosen for tractable turnaround; results are consistent across
seeds but a larger-scale campaign (3600~s episodes, 300 episodes, five seeds)
would tighten confidence intervals. Reported emissions are simulator estimates.
Finally, on heterogeneous traffic the learned agent does not yet exceed the
actuated baseline at the current budget.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}
Natural extensions include protected left-turn phases with a larger discrete
action set; multi-intersection and arterial coordination via multi-agent RL;
training a single generalist policy across all demand regimes rather than one per
scenario; richer state encoders (convolutional or attention-based) and longer
training to close the heterogeneous-traffic gap to actuated control; and
algorithmic upgrades such as prioritized experience replay and distributional
value estimation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented a Dueling Double DQN controller for an isolated four-way
intersection, trained in SUMO and evaluated rigorously against fixed-time and
actuated baselines across three demand regimes with multi-seed statistics. The
controller reduces average waiting time by 75.5--82.6\% versus fixed-time and by
up to 38\% versus actuated control, with matched throughput and substantially
lower estimated emissions. A PCU-weighted state and reward extension for
heterogeneous traffic improves the agent by 23.9\% over an unweighted counterpart
while reducing variance. The complete pipeline is containerized and reproducible
with a single command, addressing the baseline-rigour, heterogeneity, and
reproducibility gaps identified at the outset.
